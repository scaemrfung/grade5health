31 Needs and Wants 30 min · What money is for ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students demonstrate how planning can support financial goals.

Needs are required for well-being (food, shelter, clothing, safety). Wants are extra. Both are human. Planning starts by telling them apart.

Materials

  • Week 31 worksheet

Warm-up (5 min)

Sort: water, video game, winter coat, extra hoodie, bus fare, concert merch. Two disagreements — talk them out.

Teach (8 min)

Needs, wants, and grey zones

  1. 1A need supports health and safety. A want is nice-to-have.
  2. 2Context matters: running shoes can be a need for gym and a want if they must be a $250 brand.
  3. 3Families have different needs. We do not rank anyone's house, lunch, or phone.
  4. 4Telling them apart is the first planning skill. You can still enjoy a want — after the needs are covered, or with a plan.

Example story (read aloud)

The class sorted: water, video game, winter coat, extra hoodie, bus fare, concert merch. Hana and Jack disagreed about the extra hoodie. Context, Mr. Fung said. A winter coat can be a need. A second hoodie with a ninety-dollar logo is often a want. Running shoes can be a need for gym and a want if they must be a two-hundred-fifty-dollar brand.

Needs support health and safety: lunch, a helmet, a charger if the family phone is how you reach a parent. Wants are extra: limited-edition cards, movie snacks. Both are human. Families have different needs. Nobody ranked anyone’s house, lunch, or phone.

Hana wrote one need: winter boots that still fit. One want she can wait for: a new pencil case with a character on it. Mixing up needs and wants makes planning harder because the want can empty the jar before the boots are covered. You can still enjoy a want after the needs are covered, or with a plan. Sample dollars only. No one had to say what their family earns.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Sort a list, then label one personal example of each without sharing money amounts out loud.

32 Saving and Financial Goals 30 min · Short-term and long-term ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students demonstrate how planning can support financial goals.

Saving means not spending now so money is there for a goal or an unexpected expense. Short-term goals can support long-term ones.

Materials

  • Week 32 worksheet
  • Calculator optional

Warm-up (5 min)

Goal: $40 soccer registration in 8 weeks. How much per week if they save evenly? ($5) What if they also want a $10 snack this weekend?

Teach (8 min)

Goals need a number and a time

  1. 1Short-term: days to a few months (a book, a field trip fee, a gift).
  2. 2Long-term: a year or more, usually more money, needs commitment (a bike, a camp, helping family with a bigger item).
  3. 3Saving strategies: decide the goal, skip some wants, put money aside on purpose (jar, envelope, account with a family adult).
  4. 4Unexpected expenses are why a small buffer helps. You will not predict everything.

Example story (read aloud)

Goal on the board: forty dollars for soccer registration in eight weeks. Even saving is five dollars a week. Mason also wanted a ten-dollar snack this weekend. The class could see the collision.

Short-term goals take days to a few months — a book, a field trip fee, a gift. Long-term goals take a year or more — a used bike, a camp. Saving means not spending now so money is there for a goal or an unexpected expense. Strategies: name the number and the time, skip some wants, put money aside on purpose with a family adult in a jar, envelope, or account.

Mason’s sample short-term goal: sixteen dollars for a used chapter-book set in four weeks, four dollars a week. Long-term: sixty dollars toward a used helmet and lights in twelve weeks, five dollars a week. One want to pause: extra vending popcorn. A short-term save helps a long-term goal because the habit — putting money aside on purpose — is the same skill, just a smaller number first.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Write one short-term and one long-term goal with a simple save-up math box. Keep dollar amounts modest and fictional if needed.

33 Simple Budgets 30 min · Income, expenses, leftover ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students demonstrate how planning can support financial goals.

A budget is a plan for earning, spending, saving, and donating over a period. Pieces: money on hand, money coming in, money going out. It can be split into needs and wants.

Materials

  • Week 33 worksheet

Warm-up (5 min)

Show a 3-line budget on the board: In $20 (birthday + chores). Needs $8. Wants $6. Save $6. Ask: is this a plan or a wish?

Teach (8 min)

Build a tiny budget

  1. 1Income: gift, chore pay, allowance — whatever is real for that student, or use the class sample numbers.
  2. 2Expenses: needs first, then wants.
  3. 3Saving and donating are choices in the plan, not leftovers that 'maybe' happen.
  4. 4If expenses beat income, cut a want or add time to the goal. Do not pretend.

Example story (read aloud)

Aaliyah copied the three-line budget: in twenty dollars, needs eight, wants six, save six. Is this a plan or a wish? A plan, if she actually follows it.

A budget is a plan for earning, spending, saving, and donating over a period. Income might be a gift, chore pay, or the class sample numbers — never a required family income. Expenses: needs first, then wants. Saving and donating are choices in the plan, not leftovers that maybe happen.

Sample: thirty dollars for a month of fun plus save. Goal: twelve dollars for a class t-shirt. Needs, a bus and snack fund, ten dollars. Left for wants: eight dollars. She filled the table with those numbers, marked save as save, and checked that it balanced: thirty equals ten plus eight plus twelve. If it did not balance, she would cut a want, not pretend. The Health idea is the plan, not long division.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Budget a class event (pizza + movie night for one person) using the sample numbers on the sheet. Then one personal or sample weekly budget.

34 Consumer Choices — What Influences Us 30 min · Ads, friends, price, quality ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students demonstrate how planning can support financial goals.

A consumer buys goods and services. Factors: needs/wants, price, quality, ads, peers, habits, values, how you feel that day.

Materials

  • Week 34 worksheet
  • One printed ad or a word-only ad you write on the board

Warm-up (5 min)

Read a hype line: 'Everyone in Grade 5 has these sneakers — don't be left out.' What is the ad selling besides shoes?

Teach (8 min)

Pause before you buy

  1. 1Influences: price, quality, need vs want, advertising, friends, packaging, 'limited time,' how hungry/tired you are.
  2. 2Quality vs brand: sometimes you pay for the logo. That can be a want.
  3. 3A good consumer question: Do I need this, can I wait, is there a cheaper option that still works, who is trying to persuade me?
  4. 4You can like a thing and still not buy it today.

Example story (read aloud)

Jack read the hype line: Everyone in Grade 5 has these sneakers — don’t be left out. The ad was selling belonging, not just shoes.

Worksheet ad: Only twenty-four hours left! Grade 5’s number-one water bottle. Only twenty-eight dollars. Your friends already have it. Influences he noticed: urgency, peers, price stated as only, and the claim of being number one. He already had a bottle that worked. He would not buy it today. A better question: Do I need this, can I wait, is there a cheaper option that still works, who is trying to persuade me?

Quality versus brand: sometimes you pay for the logo. That can be a want. You can like a thing and still not buy it today. Nobody roasted students who already owned a hyped bottle. The skill was noticing influence, not purity. Two factors that influence consumer choice: ads and friends — also price, quality, mood, and whether it is a need.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Tear apart one ad (board or worksheet) and judge one purchase scenario.

35 Planning: Earn, Spend, Save, Donate 30 min · Four money jobs ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students demonstrate how planning can support financial goals.

Planning can include earning, spending (needs then wants), saving, and donating. Not every week includes all four — the plan decides.

Materials

  • Week 35 worksheet

Warm-up (5 min)

Four corners of the board: Earn / Spend / Save / Donate. Students give one Grade 5 example for each.

Teach (8 min)

A plan is a sequence

  1. 1Earn: chores, small jobs with family permission, gifts you choose to treat as income.
  2. 2Spend: needs first.
  3. 3Save: toward a goal or a buffer.
  4. 4Donate: time (Week 1 volunteering) or money, if you choose and can.
  5. 5Planning supports well-being: less panic, more fairness to future-you, room to be generous on purpose.

Example story (read aloud)

Four corners: Earn, Spend, Save, Donate. Meera’s Grade 5 examples: earn — folding laundry for two dollars with family permission; spend — replacing worn mitts; save — a jar for a used helmet; donate — two dollars to the class fundraiser or time stacking chairs. Week 1 still counts.

She planned twenty-five dollars of sample money. Goal: eight dollars toward a used bike helmet, because safety is a need. Snacks as a want. Maybe two dollars to the fundraiser. Table: save eight for the helmet; need six for mitts if hers were holey; want nine for snacks; donate two. That is twenty-five. If she only had eighteen, she would cut snacks first and protect the helmet.

Donating is optional in real life. Nobody moralized kids who cannot give money. Time still counts. Planning supports well-being: less panic, more fairness to future-you, room to be generous on purpose.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Plan $25 of sample money across the four jobs for a month. Must include at least one need and some saving.

36 My Financial Plan 30 min · Unit wrap ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students demonstrate how planning can support financial goals.

Put needs/wants, a goal, a simple budget, and one consumer pause into one page. This is unit evidence.

Materials

  • Week 36 worksheet

Warm-up (5 min)

One-sentence review: A plan tells future-you what this-week-you decided.

Teach (8 min)

The through-line

  1. 1Needs before wants.
  2. 2Goals need a number and a time.
  3. 3A budget is the written plan.
  4. 4Consumer pauses stop ads and friends from running the plan.
  5. 5Planning supports well-being — not becoming rich in Grade 5, but feeling less stuck.

Example story (read aloud)

A plan, Lucas said, tells future-you what this-week-you decided. Needs before wants. Goals need a number and a time. A budget is the written plan. Consumer pauses stop ads and friends from running the plan. Planning supports well-being — not becoming rich in Grade 5, but feeling less stuck.

His one-pager used sample money. Need: gym runners that still fit. Want: a new game skin. Short-term goal: twenty-four dollars for a field trip fee in six weeks. Mini-budget: in thirty, needs twelve, wants six, save twelve.

Consumer influence to watch: limited-time language. How planning supports goals and well-being: the twelve-dollar save actually reaches the fee, and he spends less energy worrying at the office. Family wealth was not scored. The page was unit evidence, simple and complete. That was week thirty-six. The class had a year of thirty-minute Health blocks in the duotang.

Financial Literacy — unit rubric (1–4)

Score once near the end of the six weeks. Use worksheets as evidence, not as 36 separate grades.

Categories (score each 1–4, then add):

  • Needs vs wants
  • Goals and saving
  • Simple budget that balances
  • Consumer pause (ads/peers/price)

Proficient: average 3 or higher, and the student can explain one idea from the unit in their own words.

1 Beginning
Work is missing, copied, or does not show the idea yet.
2 Developing
Some of the idea is there, but examples are thin or mixed up.
3 Proficient
Clear, complete work that matches the outcome. A classmate could learn from it.
4 Extending
Goes further with a specific, honest example and a next step that fits real life.
