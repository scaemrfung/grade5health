13 Responsibility and the Impact of Choices 30 min · Actions have consequences ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students analyze responsibility and consider the impact on well-being.

Responsibility means owning a choice, naming who it affects, and repairing if needed.

Materials

  • Week 13 worksheet
  • Timer

Warm-up (5 min)

Chain: a backpack in the aisle. Who besides the owner is affected? List three impacts.

Teach (8 min)

Responsibility is not the same as blame

  1. 1Responsible = I own my choice, I can name who it affects, I can repair.
  2. 2Impacts can be physical, social-emotional, or practical (time, money, trust).
  3. 3Factors: hurry, audience, not knowing the rule, mood, trying to be funny.
  4. 4An apology without a changed next choice is incomplete.

Example story (read aloud)

Owen left his backpack in the aisle for a second. Three people were affected: Ms. Patel nearly tripped, the class lost a minute, and Owen’s water bottle rolled under a desk and leaked on someone’s duo-tang.

Responsibility, the class practised, is not the same as a shame pile-on. Owen owned the choice, named the impacts — physical, almost a fall; practical, time and a wet booklet; social, the owner of the duo-tang felt dismissed — and repaired. He picked up the bag, wiped the floor, and offered to recopy the ruined page at lunch.

They traced hypotheticals, not real class incidents. Forwarding a screenshot of a private message: trust is affected; repair is delete, apologize, and not do it again. Sitting at the nut-aware table with a granola bar: a classmate’s safety is affected; you move. Promising to walk a younger buddy then going to the park: the buddy and their family are affected; you show up or you get an adult to cover. An apology without a changed next choice, Owen wrote, is incomplete.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Trace three scenarios: choice → who is affected → responsible next step. No real class incidents as examples.

14 Laws, Rules, Practices, and Protocols 30 min · Why the structures exist ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students analyze responsibility and consider the impact on well-being.

Identify laws, rules, practices, and protocols that support safety and well-being.

Materials

  • Week 14 worksheet

Warm-up (5 min)

Students guess the difference between a law, a school rule, a practice, and a protocol before you teach it.

Teach (8 min)

Four layers

  1. 1Law: whole country/province (seatbelts; it is against the law to threaten someone).
  2. 2School rule: our building (phones away, hall passes).
  3. 3Practice: a habit when no one is watching (walk on the right, wash hands).
  4. 4Protocol: agreed steps for a specific situation (fire drill: line, silent, attendance clipboard).
  5. 5If a rule is confusing, ask — do not skip it.

Example story (read aloud)

Fatima guessed that protocol meant being polite. Close, but not it. Mr. Fung stacked four layers: a law, like seatbelts, for the whole country; a school rule, like phones in lockers; a practice, like walking on the right when no one is watching; and a protocol, the agreed steps for a specific situation.

They walked a fire drill in words: silent line, attendance clipboard, wait for all-clear. Fatima wrote who it protects: every person in the building, including visitors and the student who is in the bathroom. If only some people follow it, the attendance count is wrong and someone might be left in a hallway.

Lockdown she labelled protocol, not a scary story. Lights off, out of sight, silent, wait for officials. The point was the steps, said calmly. Fatima asked what to do if a rule is confusing. Ask, Mr. Fung said. Do not skip it. A protocol is a sequence. A rule is a boundary. Both protect people, but a protocol tells your feet what to do in order when your brain is noisy.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Classify examples, then write one school protocol in order.

15 Personal Safety and Trusted Adults 30 min · Safe, unsafe, uncomfortable ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students analyze responsibility and consider the impact on well-being.

Personal safety includes freedom from physical harm, illness, and emotional harm. Name trusted adults.

Materials

  • Week 15 worksheet
  • Question box

Warm-up (5 min)

Sort: Safe / Uncomfortable / Unsafe. You sort 'an adult asking you to keep a special secret about touching' as unsafe.

Teach (8 min)

Personal safety plan

  1. 1Safe: you can learn, play, and rest without harm.
  2. 2Uncomfortable: something feels off. You can say no, leave, and tell an adult even if you are not sure.
  3. 3Unsafe: harm or a body/privacy boundary being broken. Tell a trusted adult the same day. You will not be in trouble for telling.
  4. 4Trusted adult: listens, does not ask you to keep unsafe secrets, can get help. Name more than one, including a school adult.
  5. 5If someone is hurt now: get an adult. Do not try to be the physical hero.

Example story (read aloud)

The class sorted cards: Safe, Uncomfortable, Unsafe. Cole put an adult asking you to keep a special secret about touching under Unsafe without debating it. Mr. Fung agreed. That is a tell-an-adult-the-same-day situation. You will not be in trouble for telling.

Cole’s personal safety card stayed private. He named three trusted adults: Mr. Fung in the classroom every day, Ms. Patel in the office before school and at lunch, and his aunt who picks him up on Thursdays. At least one school adult. A trusted adult listens, does not ask you to keep unsafe secrets, and can get help.

Uncomfortable, he wrote, is when something feels off but you are not sure — a joke that goes too far, a dare to hide in the bathroom. You can say no, leave, and tell an adult even if you are not sure. If someone is hurt now, get an adult. Do not try to be the physical hero. Telling to keep someone safe is responsible, not tattling.

16 Group Safety and Responsible Leadership 30 min · Looking after more than yourself ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students analyze responsibility and consider the impact on well-being.

Responsible leadership supports the safety and well-being of self and others.

Materials

  • Week 16 worksheet

Warm-up (5 min)

Field trip, partner missing. Responsible leader vs bossy vs silent. Two ideas each.

Teach (8 min)

Leadership here means safety

  1. 1Notice, speak up, get an adult, include people, follow the protocol even if friends roll their eyes.
  2. 2Not: taking over, humiliating someone, or keeping a dangerous secret to be loyal.
  3. 3Loyalty to friends does not beat safety. A true friend gets help.

Example story (read aloud)

On the imaginary field trip, a partner was missing. Yuki practised three versions. Bossy: yell at the group and name-call. Silent: hope someone else noticed. Responsible: tell the teacher immediately, stay with the protocol, do not send friends searching the parking lot alone.

They judged four scenes. A classmate left on the bench every game: invite them in or ask the coach to rotate. A dare to climb a roof: get an adult, leave, do not keep it as a loyalty secret. A hidden inhaler: that is urgent medical safety — tell an adult now, not it was a joke. A group chat rating people: refuse, tell an adult, do not pile on.

Loyalty without safety, Yuki wrote, is not friendship because a true friend gets help. Responsible leadership at Grade 5 means notice, speak up, include people, and follow the protocol even if friends roll their eyes. It does not mean taking over or humiliating someone.

17 Digital Privacy and Online Responsibility 30 min · Digital citizenship ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students analyze responsibility and consider the impact on well-being.

Privacy intrusion, bullying, misinformation, sharing without permission. Personal information is not public just because a device is handy.

Materials

  • Week 17 worksheet

Warm-up (5 min)

Mark Share / Ask first / Never: full name, school, gym photo, address, screenshot of a private chat.

Teach (8 min)

Privacy is a safety skill

  1. 1Do not post the set: real name + school + photo + location + schedule.
  2. 2Passwords, home address, student ID stay private.
  3. 3Photos and screenshots need permission. 'They were in the background' is not permission.
  4. 4Uncomfortable message or image: do not reply to impress anyone; tell a trusted adult. You will not be in trouble for telling.
  5. 5Sharing a rumour is still a choice you own.

Example story (read aloud)

Zara marked the warm-up quickly. Full name plus school plus photo plus location: never as a set. Gym photo: ask first. Address: never. Screenshot of a private chat: never without permission — usually never.

A photo of a classmate in a change room: keep private, actually never take it. Team win photo after asking everyone: OK. Home address in a game chat: keep private. Homework reminder in Google Classroom: OK.

Then the case. Someone in a game asked Zara to switch to a private app and keep it secret from her parents. She wrote: I do not switch. I do not keep that secret. I tell a trusted adult the same day — a parent or Mr. Fung. I will not be in trouble for telling. Privacy is a safety skill, not a vibe. Passwords, student ID, and someone else’s photo are not public just because a device is handy. School accounts, Mr. Fung added, are not private from teachers and admin.

Privacy sort plus one 'secret app' case. Grade 5 only. No graphic content.

18 Consent and Refusal — Respecting Yes and No 30 min · Permission skills ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students analyze responsibility and consider the impact on well-being.

Ask, wait, accept no. Grade 5 contexts: space, photos, games, borrowing, hugs, group chats. Not a lesson on sexual activity.

Materials

  • Week 18 worksheet

Warm-up (5 min)

Ask to borrow a pencil. Wait for a real yes. Then model taking it after a no and freeze.

Teach (8 min)

Consent in Grade 5 language

  1. 1Consent = clear permission. Silence or 'I guess' under pressure is not a yes.
  2. 2Ask: 'Is it okay if I ___?' Wait. Accept no without teasing, sulking, or doing it anyway.
  3. 3You can change your mind. A yes can become a no. The no counts immediately.
  4. 4Practise: hugs, rough play, borrowing, posting a photo, adding someone to a chat, standing close in line.
  5. 5If someone ignores your no: leave, tell a trusted adult. If you missed someone's no: stop, apologize, change.
  6. 6We are not discussing sexual activity.

Example story (read aloud)

Mr. Fung asked to borrow Ben’s pencil. He waited for a real yes. Then he modelled taking it after a no and froze the scene. The class felt the difference in their shoulders.

Consent in Grade 5 language means clear permission. Silence or I guess under pressure is not a yes. Ask: Is it okay if I take a photo of your poster for the project? Wait. If yes, take only that photo. If no, accept it without teasing, sulking, or doing it anyway. A hug to say congratulations: ask; a wave or a nice job is a fine substitute. Adding someone to a group chat: ask; if no, leave them off.

Ben practised refusal words he would actually say: No thanks. I don’t want a hug. Please don’t post that. If someone ignores a no: leave, tell a trusted adult. If you missed someone’s no: stop, apologize, change. They were not discussing sexual activity. They were discussing photos, hugs, borrowing, and chats. They didn’t say no, Ben wrote, is not the same as consent because a yes has to be clear.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Write ask / yes / no scripts. Optional 2-minute stand-up: ask to see a drawing and accept no.

Safety — unit rubric (1–4)

Score once near the end of the six weeks. Use worksheets as evidence, not as 36 separate grades.

Categories (score each 1–4, then add):

  • Responsibility and repair
  • Safety structures (rules/protocols)
  • Digital privacy
  • Asking and accepting yes/no

Proficient: average 3 or higher, and the student can explain one idea from the unit in their own words.

1 Beginning
Work is missing, copied, or does not show the idea yet.
2 Developing
Some of the idea is there, but examples are thin or mixed up.
3 Proficient
Clear, complete work that matches the outcome. A classmate could learn from it.
4 Extending
Goes further with a specific, honest example and a next step that fits real life.
