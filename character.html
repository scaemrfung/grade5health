1 Volunteering — Time, Talent, and Energy 30 min · Community contribution ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students reflect and relate life experiences to perseverance and well-being.

Focus today: volunteering is donating time, talent, and energy to benefit people and community. It can build purpose and belonging.

Materials

  • Printed Week 1 worksheet
  • Board: Time / Talent / Energy
  • Timer

Warm-up (5 min)

Ask: “When has someone given their time to help you — a coach, a library volunteer, a lunch supervisor, a family member?” Two students share. Write Time, Talent, Energy on the board.

Teach (8 min)

What volunteering is (and is not)

  1. 1Volunteering means you give time, talent, or energy to help people or a place — not because you are paid, but because it helps.
  2. 2Time = hours you show up (shelving books, picking litter). Talent = a skill you share (reading with a buddy, drawing a poster). Energy = effort when the job is ordinary (stacking chairs, packing food hampers).
  3. 3It is not the same as a chore you must do at home, and it is not a punishment. It is a choice to contribute.
  4. 4Grade 5 examples that fit our community: school recycling team, reading buddy, helping at a community league event, packing kits for a food bank drive, walking dogs at a shelter with a family adult.

Why it connects to well-being

  1. 1Helping can grow a sense of purpose (“I am useful here”) and belonging (“I am part of this group”).
  2. 2You do not need a huge project. One regular, honest contribution is enough to start.

Example story (read aloud)

On a Thursday after school, Maya stayed to help Ms. Patel restock the lost-and-found. The bin smelled like wet mittens. Maya did not get paid. She folded hoodies, paired mittens, and printed a sign: Please take what is yours. Liam from Grade 3 found his toque and grinned like it was a gift.

Maya is not the loudest kid in the gym. She is good at noticing what is messy and making it neat. That is a talent. The time she gave was twenty minutes. The energy was the part where she kept folding even after her friends left for the bus loop.

The next morning the hallway felt calmer. Maya told the class she did not save the world — she just made the bin usable. Mr. Fung wrote Time, Talent, and Energy on the board and pointed at her story. Volunteering is a choice to contribute, not a chore and not a punishment. Maya sat a little taller. She had a place to be useful, and the Grade 3s had dry hats.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Students complete the volunteer map on the worksheet. They do not need a real placement today — they connect skills they already have to a possible contribution. Circulate. If a student says “I have no talents,” name one you have seen (holds the door, explains math, notices when someone is left out).

2 Life Experiences Shape Who We Are 30 min · Experiences and understanding ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students reflect and relate life experiences to perseverance and well-being.

Focus today: life experiences can inform strengths and preferences. They shape how we understand events and how we respond.

Materials

  • Week 2 worksheet
  • Board: Experience → What I learned → How I respond

Warm-up (5 min)

Read: “Two students both miss the same soccer goal. One laughs and tries again. One sits out.” Ask: What life experiences might sit behind those two responses? Take two ideas. No names, no roasting.

Teach (8 min)

Experiences leave traces

  1. 1A life experience is something that happens to you or that you take part in: a move, a new school, a sport, a loss, a success, a family tradition, a tough test, a kind teacher.
  2. 2Experiences can be individual (you practised piano alone) or shared (your class put on a concert).
  3. 3Over time, experiences can teach skills, strengths, and preferences (“I like working with my hands”; “I need quiet before a test”).
  4. 4They can also shape how we read a new situation. If you have been left out before, a group project can feel risky. If you have been coached well, a hard skill can feel possible.

Example story (read aloud)

Noah used to freeze during spelling bees at his old school in Sherwood Park. One year he blanked on a simple word and sat down shaking. At this school, Ms. Grant lets students practise out loud with a partner before the Friday quiz. Noah still feels the old panic, but now he asks for the practise time instead of pretending he is fine.

When the class missed a wide shot in intramural soccer, two kids reacted differently. Noah laughed and asked for another try. A teammate sat out. Noah thought about the spelling bee. Missing once had taught him that a second try feels less huge if someone walks you through it.

He wrote on his private map: experience — freezing in a bee; skill — asking for practise; preference — a quiet rehearsal, not being put on the spot first. He did not share the bee story out loud. He only shared, I like a warm-up before I perform. That was enough. Life experiences leave traces, even ordinary ones like a bee, a move, or learning to ride a bike on a gravel alley in Fort Saskatchewan.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Students complete a private experience map. Tell them: you will not read these aloud. Share only if you choose the last box, which is optional to share. Protect privacy — no forcing trauma stories. Everyday examples are enough (learning to ride a bike, joining choir, moving houses).

3 Perseverance — Staying With Hard Things 30 min · Effort and well-being ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students reflect and relate life experiences to perseverance and well-being.

Focus today: perseverance is finding ways to continue to improve skills and stay motivated during difficult situations. It includes effort, courage, commitment, and belief that you can get better.

Materials

  • Week 3 worksheet
  • Board: Effort · Courage · Commitment · Belief

Warm-up (5 min)

Show a 10-second “first try vs later try” story (tying shoes, a math strategy, a jump rope trick). Ask: What did the person do between try one and try ten? List verbs: practised, asked for help, rested, tried a new way.

Teach (8 min)

Name perseverance without fake cheerfulness

  1. 1Perseverance is not “never feel stuck.” It is staying with a hard thing long enough to improve — sometimes by changing the plan.
  2. 2Four supports: effort (you show up), courage (you try while nervous), commitment (you return after a break), belief (you think improvement is possible).
  3. 3Perseverance can be individual (you keep decoding a book) or group/community (a class fundraiser that takes weeks).
  4. 4It can lead to positive feelings when a goal moves forward — for you or for a community. It can also be tiring. Rest is part of the plan, not quitting.

Example story (read aloud)

Aisha’s recorder squeaked on the high B. Band was in twelve minutes. She wanted to shove the instrument in her backpack and claim a sore throat. Instead she opened her case, played the bar slowly, and asked Mr. Kwan if she could sit in the second row today so she could watch his fingers.

Effort looked like six extra minutes at lunch. Courage looked like playing the squeaky note in front of two friends. Commitment looked like going home, resting her jaw, and coming back Thursday. Belief looked like writing in her agenda: High B gets cleaner if I slow down.

By Friday the note was not perfect. It was less of a duck. Aisha did not make a hero speech. She told the class perseverance is staying with a hard thing long enough to improve — sometimes by changing the plan, not by pretending it is easy. Rest, she added, is part of the plan. Her protective factor was Mr. Kwan’s fingering chart taped inside the case.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Students pick one real hard thing from school, sport, music, friendship, or a chore. They map what perseverance could look like this week — not a hero speech.

4 Strengths, Skills, and Community Contribution 30 min · From me to we ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students reflect and relate life experiences to perseverance and well-being.

Focus today: every individual has the ability to contribute in different ways. Connect personal knowledge and skills to volunteering opportunities.

Materials

  • Week 4 worksheet
  • Optional: list of real school volunteer jobs

Warm-up (5 min)

Rapid list on the board: quiet strengths (notices litter, remembers names, good with younger kids, patient with tech). Students add two more. Point out: loud leadership is only one kind.

Teach (8 min)

Match skills to a real need

  1. 1Communities need many kinds of contribution: organizers, greeters, lifters, artists, translators, people who stay after to put things away.
  2. 2Your life experiences (Weeks 1–3) are clues. If you have moved schools, you know how a new student feels. If you play an instrument, you can help at a concert set-up.
  3. 3A good match is useful and realistic. “Become famous and donate millions” is not a Grade 5 plan. “Help the office stuff envelopes on Friday” is.
  4. 4Contribution can be short (one event) or ongoing (a weekly job). Both count.

Example story (read aloud)

Liam is not the kid who grabs the microphone on assembly day. He remembers names. When a new student, Priya, arrived from another Elk Island school, Liam showed her the boot rack and the quiet side of the playground.

Mr. Fung asked the class to match skills to real needs. Liam wrote: skill — remembering names and noticing who is left out; need — new students feeling lost in a big building; idea — a Friday welcome walk with the office’s permission.

The first Friday felt awkward. He almost hid in the library. Then he thought of last week — commitment includes coming back after a wobble. He asked Ms. Patel if he could be on the new-kid tour crew with one other student. She said yes, twenty minutes, once a week if needed. Loud leadership is only one kind. Greeters, translators, people who put chairs away — the school needs those too. Liam’s realistic try was local, not become famous and donate millions.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Students complete a match-up table: three skills, three possible contributions, one they could actually try at school this month. If your school has real jobs, post them.

5 Protective Factors That Help Us Keep Going 30 min · Supports for perseverance ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students reflect and relate life experiences to perseverance and well-being.

Focus today: identify protective factors that can support perseverance (people, habits, places, and tools that make it more likely you will keep going).

Materials

  • Week 5 worksheet
  • Board: People · Habits · Places · Tools

Warm-up (5 min)

Scenario: “Jordan wants to quit band after a rough practice.” What could make it more likely Jordan continues in a healthy way? Collect four answers and sort them into People / Habits / Places / Tools.

Teach (8 min)

Protective factors are not magic

  1. 1A protective factor is something that supports you when a task is hard: a person who believes you, a habit like sleep, a place that feels safe, a tool like a checklist or a timer.
  2. 2They do not remove the hard thing. They make perseverance more possible.
  3. 3Examples: a coach who teaches the next small step; a bedtime that is actually kept; the library corner; breaking a project into boxes; asking for a worked example.
  4. 4Different people need different supports. Copying a friend’s factor only works if it fits your life.

Example story (read aloud)

Jordan wanted to quit band after a rough practice. The class sorted ideas into People, Habits, Places, and Tools. Priya thought about her own hard thing: long division still tangled her up on quizzes.

People: her aunt who says show me the stuck step, and Mr. Fung’s extra-help Tuesday. Habits: sleep before a quiz instead of late-night gaming. Places: the library corner at lunch, where the chairs do not scrape. Tools: a checklist that splits a question into boxes, and a timer so she does not freeze for ten minutes on item one.

Priya copied none of Jordan’s factors. Jordan needed a section leader. Priya needed the checklist. Protective factors are not magic. They do not erase the hard thing. They make perseverance more possible. She circled one factor for the next seven days: the Tuesday extra-help. Just try harder, she wrote, is weaker because it does not tell me the next small step.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Students identify their own protective factors and mark which ones they could use this week on the hard thing from Week 3 (or a new one).

6 Relating Life Experiences to Well-Being 30 min · Unit wrap ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students reflect and relate life experiences to perseverance and well-being.

Focus today: pull the unit together. Describe effects of perseverance on well-being, and relate a life experience to how you stick with hard things.

Materials

  • Week 6 worksheet
  • Unit rubric (on this page)

Warm-up (5 min)

Three words on the board: volunteering, experience, perseverance. Students turn and tell a partner how the three connect, in one breath. Listen in; steal one strong sentence to share (no names unless they offer).

Teach (8 min)

The through-line

  1. 1Life experiences teach us skills and how we see challenges.
  2. 2Perseverance is how we stay with a challenge long enough to grow — using protective factors, not just grit speeches.
  3. 3Volunteering is one way those skills can help a community, which can support purpose and belonging (well-being).
  4. 4Well-being here means feeling able to handle life a bit better: mood, health habits, relationships, and a sense that you can contribute.

Example story (read aloud)

Jordan, Priya, and Liam sat with three words on the board: volunteering, experience, perseverance. Jordan had almost quit band. The experience of a kind section leader changed how he saw a bad practice — not as proof he should stop, but as a signal to use a protective factor.

He wrote his reflection in ordinary language. Life experience: moving from a smaller school and feeling lost in the gym. Perseverance: still joining intramurals, using Liam’s Friday welcome idea in reverse — he now greets other new kids. Protective factor: the library corner. Contribution: stacking chairs after the community-league movie night his family already attends.

Well-being, the class decided, is not a poster of smiling kids. It is feeling a bit more able to handle life: mood, habits, relationships, and a sense that you can contribute. Jordan’s sentence was plain: When I stick with band using help, I sleep better on practice nights because I am not dreading them.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Short written reflection used as the main unit evidence. Quiet work. You circulate with the rubric categories in mind.

Character Development — unit rubric (1–4)

Score once near the end of the six weeks. Use worksheets as evidence, not as 36 separate grades.

Categories (score each 1–4, then add):

  • Reflection on life experiences
  • Connection to perseverance
  • Community contribution / volunteering
  • Clear exit thinking

Proficient: average 3 or higher, and the student can explain one idea from the unit in their own words.

1 Beginning
Work is missing, copied, or does not show the idea yet.
2 Developing
Some of the idea is there, but examples are thin or mixed up.
3 Proficient
Clear, complete work that matches the outcome. A classmate could learn from it.
4 Extending
Goes further with a specific, honest example and a next step that fits real life.
