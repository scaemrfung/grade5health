Healthy Eating · Week 23 of 36

Cultural, Traditional, and Medicinal Food Practices

Including FNMI food knowledge · 30 minutes

Unit packet

30-minute block

05:00Warm-up

Today’s focus. Cultural, traditional, and medicinal food practices, including First Nations, Métis, and Inuit, contribute to well-being. Respect, not a recipe test.

Materials

  • Week 23 worksheet
  • Optional: a locally appropriate, school-approved FNMI resource (not a random internet list)
5 min

Warm-up

Name one food that means something in your family (celebration, comfort, everyday). No ranking. No “weird food” talk.

8 min

Food is knowledge, not a costume

  1. 1Many families have cultural food practices: what is eaten, when, with whom, and how it is prepared. These can support belonging and well-being.
  2. 2Traditional practices are passed down. Medicinal practices use foods or plants as part of staying well — this is knowledge, not a Grade 5 diagnosis kit.
  3. 3First Nations, Métis, and Inuit food knowledge in Alberta may include (examples, not a complete list): berries, fish, moose or other hunted foods, bannock, tea, gardens, and teachings about taking only what is needed. Specific nations (Cree, Dene, Blackfoot, Métis, and others) have their own knowledge. Do not flatten them into one “Native food.”
  4. 4Canada’s Food Guide has complementary First Nations, Inuit, and Métis healthy eating resources. The 2019 plate is one tool. It is not the only valid way to eat.
  5. 5We listen with respect. We do not treat sacred or medicinal knowledge as a craft activity. We do not require students to share family food if they do not want to.

Example story (read aloud)

Leila’s family eats rice together most Fridays. That is her practice, and she did not have to say it out loud. The class made two quiet columns: What I know from my family, and What I am learning with respect.

A school-approved guest resource described how some Métis families smoke fish and how some Cree teachings connect berries to a season, not to a supermarket week. The point was relationship with land and people, not “try this at home without context.”

Leila wrote: my family’s Friday rice is cultural. Bannock in a textbook is not mine to claim. I can respect it without pretending it is my tradition. Medicinal does not mean I get to pick a plant on the field trip and experiment. Knowledge Keepers decide what is shared.

Faith connection · optional

Genesis 2:15

“Yahweh God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it.”

Care for land, and taking only what is needed, is older than a grocery list. We listen to First Nations, Métis, and Inuit food knowledge with respect. We do not copy it as a costume or claim it as our own story.

Not Alberta PEW. Read after the story if it fits this class. Skip it if it does not. Student worksheets stay secular.

12 min

Activity / worksheet

Respect notes: one family practice (optional), one FNMI idea learned with respect, one “I will not” (stereotype or taking knowledge).
