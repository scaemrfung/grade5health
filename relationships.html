7 How Experiences Shape Perspectives 30 min · What a perspective is ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students acknowledge and connect perspectives of self and others through communication and listening skills.

Focus today: a perspective is how someone understands, thinks, and feels about a situation. Experiences, personal factors, and environment can shape it.

Materials

  • Week 7 worksheet
  • Simple scenario cards (printed on the worksheet)

Warm-up (5 min)

Hold up an object (mug, ball). Students on the left describe only what they see; students on the right do the same. Point out: same object, different view. That is a tiny model of perspective.

Teach (8 min)

Perspective is not the same as “right vs wrong”

  1. 1Perspective = how a person understands a situation from where they stand — their experiences, feelings, and information.
  2. 2Two people can both be honest and still see a recess problem differently.
  3. 3Factors that influence perspective: past experiences, culture and family, how tired or hungry you are, where you were standing, what you heard first.
  4. 4We acknowledge a perspective when we can say it fairly, even if we do not agree.

Example story (read aloud)

Mr. Fung held up a chipped Elk Island mug. Students on the window side described the handle. Students on the door side described a crack they could see and the others could not. Same mug, different view.

Then he read Scenario A. Sam had the soccer ball. Riley said Sam always hogged it last year. Sam had just joined the school this month and did not know about the rotation. Elena wrote Sam’s perspective without picking a winner: Sam thinks it is an open game; nobody told them the rule. Mateo wrote Riley’s: last year felt unfair, so this year already looks like the same pattern.

Nobody in the class crowned a hero. Perspective, Elena said, is how a person understands a situation from where they stand — experiences, feelings, information. Two people can both be honest. Acknowledging a perspective means saying it fairly even if you do not agree yet. Mateo added an environmental factor: if you were stuck as goalie the whole recess, your view of just one more play changes.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Students read two short recess scenarios and write each person’s likely perspective. No picking a winner yet — that is a later week.

8 Listening So Others Feel Heard 30 min · Listening skills ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students acknowledge and connect perspectives of self and others through communication and listening skills.

Focus today: listening skills that support understanding another person’s perspective — not listening to reload your own point.

Materials

  • Week 8 worksheet
  • Partner seating

Warm-up (5 min)

Model two 20-second listens. First: you interrupt, look away, and say “same, but me.” Second: you face the speaker, wait, and paraphrase. Ask which one made the speaker feel heard.

Teach (8 min)

Four listening moves

  1. 1Body: face the person, devices down, don’t fake-listen while scanning the room.
  2. 2Wait: let them finish. Silence of two seconds is allowed.
  3. 3Paraphrase: “So you felt ___ because ___.” Check: “Did I get that right?”
  4. 4Curious question: one question that is not a trap. “What happened next?” beats “Why would you do that?”

Example story (read aloud)

Harper’s partner, Dev, talked for forty-five seconds about a time the art supplies vanished before his group could start. Harper’s first instinct was to jump in with her own story about missing markers. She bit that back. She faced Dev, waited two seconds after he finished, and tried a paraphrase: So you felt rushed because the bin was empty and you thought we didn’t save you anything?

Dev said, Partly. I also thought the teacher would blame our group. Harper asked one curious question that was not a trap: What happened next? not Why didn’t you just grab them earlier?

They swapped. Harper talked about a lining-up moment that felt unfair — no classmate names. Dev looked at her, did not interrupt, and wrote her view in his words. On the worksheet Harper checked You heard me: partly, because Dev had skipped the part about her stomach dropping. They tried once more. The second paraphrase landed. Mr. Fung’s coaching line stayed on the board: Say their view, not your verdict. Harper wrote that waiting was the hardest move. Silence felt like failing. It was actually the work.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Pairs: Speaker has 45 seconds on “a time something felt unfair at school (no names).” Listener uses the four moves, then writes the paraphrase on the worksheet. Swap. You time it. If odd number, a triad with an observer.

9 Communicating With Respect 30 min · Clear, kind, specific ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students acknowledge and connect perspectives of self and others through communication and listening skills.

Focus today: respectful communication skills — I-messages, specific requests, tone and body language that match the words.

Materials

  • Week 9 worksheet

Warm-up (5 min)

Read two lines in a flat voice then a sharp voice: “I wasn’t finished with that.” Same words, different impact. Students vote: which one invites a conversation?

Teach (8 min)

A respectful message has three parts

  1. 1What I noticed (fact): “The markers were dumped in the bin.”
  2. 2How it affected me (feeling or need): “I couldn’t find a working one.”
  3. 3A clear ask: “Please put lids on and stand them up.”
  4. 4Avoid you-always attacks, sarcasm, and public call-outs. Respect includes volume, timing (not in front of a crowd if it can wait), and accepting that the other person may need a minute.

Example story (read aloud)

Someone dumped markers in the bin with no lids. Dev’s blunt line in his head was, You always wreck our group. He practised the three-part message instead, first in a flat voice, then in a sharp one. Same words, different impact. The class voted: the flat, specific version invited a conversation.

Dev wrote: I noticed the markers were dumped without lids. It affected me because I could not find a working one for the map labels. Could you put lids on and stand them up?

He crossed out thief and stupid idea on the rewrite table. Respect includes volume, timing, and not calling someone out in front of a crowd if it can wait. Dev’s real situation was a borrowed eraser that never came back. He decided to ask at recess, not during the mini-lesson. A specific ask works better than a put-down because the other person can actually do the thing. Stop being awful is not a job. Please return the eraser after math is.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Rewrite three blunt lines into respectful three-part messages. Then write one real line they might need this week (optional to use in real life).

10 When Perspectives Differ 30 min · Shared understanding ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students acknowledge and connect perspectives of self and others through communication and listening skills.

Focus today: identify ways to reach shared understandings when perspectives or opinions in a group are different. Shared understanding is not always full agreement.

Materials

  • Week 10 worksheet

Warm-up (5 min)

Class split by a low-stakes choice: window open vs closed, or extra-recess game. Two volunteers state their view. Ask: do we need one winner, or a plan both can live with?

Teach (8 min)

Four paths when we disagree

  1. 1Clarify: maybe we heard different facts. Check the information.
  2. 2Both/and: we can do one thing now and the other next time (take turns).
  3. 3Third option: a new plan that uses a piece of each idea.
  4. 4Agree to pause: if it is not urgent, wait and ask an adult. Shared understanding can be “we see it differently, and here is how we will work anyway.”
  5. 5What does not work: talking over, recruiting a pile-on, or pretending you agree when you don’t — that just leaks out later.

Example story (read aloud)

The group poster was due Friday. Avery wanted lots of drawings. Blake wanted mostly text so it was readable from the back of the gym. Casey wanted to split up and not talk. The teacher said it had to be one poster, all names on it.

Sienna’s trio tried the four paths. They clarified the actual size of the paper. They did not need a winner. Third option: a bold title and two large drawings on the left, short bullet facts on the right, with Casey in charge of cutting and spacing — a job that did not require extra talking.

Shared understanding, Sienna told the class, is not everyone getting their first choice. It is a plan people can live with. Casey was included without being forced to brainstorm out loud. Avery still got colour. Blake still got readable words. What would not have worked: talking over Casey, recruiting friends to vote Avery off the poster, or pretending to agree and then scribbling extra cartoons at home.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Students take one group-conflict scenario and propose a shared-understanding plan. They must name which path they used.

11 Working Together on a Shared Task 30 min · Practise the skills ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students acknowledge and connect perspectives of self and others through communication and listening skills.

Focus today: demonstrate respectful communication while working with others on a short task or challenge.

Materials

  • Week 11 worksheet
  • One envelope per trio with 8–10 paper strips (or just the planning sheet if you want zero prep)

Warm-up (5 min)

Roles on the board: Facilitator (keeps turns), Recorder (writes), Checker (reads the goal aloud). 30 seconds to recall last week’s four paths.

Teach (8 min)

The 8-minute challenge

  1. 1In trios, plan a 15-minute indoor recess activity that includes people who like sports and people who do not. You will not run the activity today — you will plan it.
  2. 2Must use: one paraphrase, one respectful ask, and a shared-understanding move if you disagree.
  3. 3If you finish early, write who does what so no one is leftover.
  4. 4I will listen for interruptions and for “you always.” Those get a reset, not a lecture.

Example story (read aloud)

Indoor recess was coming and the gym was booked. Theo’s trio had eight minutes to plan a fifteen-minute activity that included kids who like sports and kids who do not. Theo was checker: he read the goal aloud twice so they would not drift into soccer or sit out.

Amara paraphrased Harper: You want something active but not a full game with winners. Theo asked, Could we do stations — wall ball for whoever wants it, and a card-building table for whoever doesn’t? That was the respectful ask. Shared understanding: take turns choosing the station, no rating who picked what.

They finished early and wrote who does what so no one was leftover. Theo would set out cones. Amara would bring the card decks from the cupboard. Harper would invite the two classmates who usually hover by the door. On his worksheet Theo admitted he almost interrupted. He wrote one thing to do differently: wait until the recorder has finished the sentence. The plan was not fancy. It was inclusive.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

8 minutes talk/plan, 4 minutes each person completes their own worksheet so quiet students still show the skill.

12 Connecting Perspectives to Healthy Relationships 30 min · Unit wrap ▼
5 warm-up 8 teach 12 worksheet 5 close

Alberta PEW Grade 5 outcome

Students acknowledge and connect perspectives of self and others through communication and listening skills.

Focus today: healthy relationships are supported when we can connect our perspective to someone else’s through listening and respectful talk.

Materials

  • Week 12 worksheet

Warm-up (5 min)

Sentence stem on the board: “A relationship feels healthier when…” Students finish in one phrase. Collect four.

Teach (8 min)

Pull the unit together

  1. 1We all have perspectives shaped by experience.
  2. 2Listening and clear, respectful talk help us connect those perspectives.
  3. 3Shared understanding lets a group keep working even when opinions differ.
  4. 4That is what “healthy relationship” looks like at Grade 5: not zero conflict — conflict handled without humiliation.

Example story (read aloud)

Amara finished the stem on the board: A relationship feels healthier when I can say my side and still hear yours. She thought of a collision that was school-appropriate and nameless: two friends both wanted the same library chair by the window. Voices got sharp. Nobody paraphrased. They just pulled the chair.

Listening would have helped. An I-message would have helped. Shared understanding might have been taking turns on odd and even days, or finding a second chair and moving it over — a third option.

Amara’s skill for next month’s group work was the pause. If it is not urgent, wait and ask an adult. Healthy relationships at Grade 5 are not zero conflict. They are conflict handled without humiliation. Connecting perspectives supports well-being, Amara wrote, because you feel safer in the group. You spend less energy bracing for a pile-on. You can still disagree. You just do not have to disappear to keep the peace.

Student activity / worksheet (12 min)

Written wrap used as unit evidence. Optional: students may describe a relationship with a sibling, teammate, or friend without names of classmates if it is sensitive.

Healthy Relationships — unit rubric (1–4)

Score once near the end of the six weeks. Use worksheets as evidence, not as 36 separate grades.

Categories (score each 1–4, then add):

  • Perspective-taking
  • Listening (paraphrase)
  • Respectful communication
  • Shared understanding in a group

Proficient: average 3 or higher, and the student can explain one idea from the unit in their own words.

1 Beginning
Work is missing, copied, or does not show the idea yet.
2 Developing
Some of the idea is there, but examples are thin or mixed up.
3 Proficient
Clear, complete work that matches the outcome. A classmate could learn from it.

Goes further with a specific, honest example and a next step that fits real life.
